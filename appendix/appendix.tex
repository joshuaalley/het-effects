\section{Replication Analysis Details}

To produce Figure 1, I downloaded every replication archive on the Harvard Dataverse for papers in the \textit{American Political Science Review}, \textit{American Journal of Political Science}, and \textit{Journal of Politics}.
Coverage runs from 2012 to 2024 and includes 1,830 archives, as deposits for 2025 and 2026 remain incomplete.
Each paper is dated by its year of publication, taken from the Dataverse citation string.
I use date of publication because journals adopted deposit mandates around 2015 \citep{Raineyetal2025}, so older articles were archived retroactively. 

Within each archive I read every file of author-written analysis code, meaning scripts in \textsf{R}, Stata, Python, Stan, and JAGS.
This excludes \texttt{.ado} files, which are third-party Stata packages rather than the author's analysis. 
Their help text contains the same syntax the analysis looks for.
The 1,830 archives contain 12,179 such files.


\subsection{Classification}

Rather than match patterns, I gave each file in full to an open-weight language model and asked whether the analysis estimates an effect that varies across units, subgroups, or contexts.
The model returns a structured judgment, the methods it identifies, and verbatim lines of code as evidence.
The model splits files longer than the context window into overlapping chunks and judges them in turn.

Pattern matching was inadequate here, and in a way that affects the trend as well as the level.
Regular expressions key on the syntax of an interaction, and that syntax changed over the period.
Stata's factor-variable notation arrived in version 11 and displaced hand-constructed product variables only gradually, so a script from 2012 is far more likely to write \texttt{gen treatXpost = treat*post} and enter the result as an ordinary regressor.
Scoring the same with pattern matching and the language model, the two approaches agree almost exactly on recent papers and diverge sharply on old ones. 
Over 2020 to 2024 both flag 67\% of papers, while over 2012 to 2015 pattern matching flags 35\% against the classifier's 58\%.


I use \texttt{openai/gpt-oss-120b}, served through DeepInfra at temperature zero.
The prompt, the full text of every request, and every raw model response are archived in the replication materials, so the classification can be verified without re-running inference.

\subsection{Validation}

I hand-adjudicated a stratified random sample of 100 file-chunks, drawn blind. I shuffled the sample and hid the model's judgment until each item was answered.
Each item displayed the relevant code with four lines of context on either side.
Weighting back to the stratum populations, precision for the measure reported in Figure 1 is above 90\%.

Two limits apply.
First, recall is not identified by this design.
Estimating recall requires knowing how often the classifier misses a heterogeneous effect entirely. Over half the corpus falls in the stratum where nothing was flagged, so there is nothing to check without reading whole files.
Figure 1 is therefore a lower bound.
Second, I do not count subgroup analysis conducted by splitting the sample and fitting the same model separately, though that estimator is algebraically identical to a fully crossed interaction for the group-specific point estimates.
I exclude it because it is the least reliably measured category. Agreement between two independently prompted models on subgroup splitting was $\kappa = 0.49$, against 0.92 for the measure reported here.


\section{Simulation Data Generating Process}

This section summarizes the data generating processes for the simulations in the main text.
All of them share one structure and differ only in two places: the probabilities used to assign the grouping variables, which control how uneven the groups are, and the distribution from which the true group treatment effects are drawn.
Every condition fixes the sample size at $n = 2000$ and varies the number of groups.

\subsection{Common Structure}

I generate $k$ binary grouping variables, where $k \in \{3, 4, 5, 6, 7\}$:
\begin{align*}
x_{ij} &\sim \text{Bernoulli}(p_j) \quad \text{for } j = 1, \ldots, k
\end{align*}

The simulation defines the groups by unique combinations of these variables, producing $2^k$ groups.
With three modifiers, there are 8 groups, while 7 modifiers produces 128 groups.
Most processes set $p_j = 0.5$ for all $j$, which makes the groups equal in expectation.
The two unbalanced processes instead space the probabilities evenly over $[0.35, 0.65]$. With $k = 7$ this is already severe, giving expected cell sizes that run from roughly one observation to roughly one hundred.


The treatment indicator is randomly assigned, also with a bernoulli draw:
\begin{align*}
T_i &\sim \text{Bernoulli}(0.5)
\end{align*}

Three control variables predict the baseline outcome but do not modify the treatment effect:
\begin{align*}
z_{i1} &\sim N(0, 1) \\
z_{i2} &\sim \text{Bernoulli}(0.5) \\
z_{i3} &\sim N(0, 1)
\end{align*}

The outcome is then generated as:
\begin{align*}
y_i &= \beta_1 z_{i1} + \beta_2 z_{i2} + \beta_3 z_{i3} + \tau_{g[i]} T_i + \epsilon_i \\
\epsilon_i &\sim N(0, \sigma_y) \quad \text{where } \sigma_y = 1
\end{align*}

I also fix the control coefficients $\boldsymbol{\beta} = (0.4, -0.3, 0.25)$.
Throughout, $\mu_\tau = 0.2$ and $\sigma_\tau = 0.3$, and each process is scaled so that the marginal standard deviation of the group effects stays near $\sigma_\tau$.
Holding that constant keeps root mean squared error comparable across processes.

Table \ref{tab:dgps} lists them.
Let $\mathbf{Z}_g$ collect the modifier values for group $g$, and let
\begin{align*}
\boldsymbol{\gamma} &= (0.20,\, -0.15,\, 0.12,\, -0.10,\, 0.08,\, -0.06,\, 0.05)
\end{align*}
truncated to the first $k$ entries.
Where a systematic component $\mathbf{Z}_g\boldsymbol{\gamma}^*$ appears it is centered and rescaled to the standard deviation shown.
In \ref{tab:dgps}, $p_j$ gives bernoulli probabilities on grouping variable values.

The last process needs some comment, because it is the one designed against the hierarchical model.
In every other process the group effects contain an idiosyncratic component that an additive interaction model cannot represent by construction, which is a comparison the hierarchical model cannot lose.
Under the exactly additive process the additive model is correctly specified, and the hierarchical model estimates a group variance that is genuinely zero.
This is why the hierarchical model is roughly three percent of root mean squared error worse in this process.


\subsection{Replication and Estimation}

Each condition uses 200 Monte Carlo replications, and the shaded bands in the figures give 95\% Monte Carlo intervals computed from the spread of per-replication root mean squared error.
Seeds are a deterministic function of the process, the number of modifiers, and the replication index, so any single condition can be reproduced in isolation.

Accuracy and errors of inference come from different estimators.
Root mean squared error requires no standard error, so it is computed over all 200 replications using restricted maximum likelihood, which is fast enough to make that many replications affordable.
Restricted maximum likelihood corresponds to no prior at all and therefore regularizes less than any half-$t$ or half-normal prior, so it is a conservative stand-in. Where the additive model loses to it, the additive model loses to the Bayesian model by more.
Sign and exaggeration errors, however, depend on which estimates are declared distinguishable from zero, and the mixed model has no convenient interval for a group treatment effect.
The prediction error is $\mathbf{x}_g'(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}} - \boldsymbol{\beta}) + (\hat{\theta}_g - \theta_g)$, and the covariance between those two terms is strongly negative, because the varying slope absorbs error in the fixed part.
Adding the two variances yields intervals covering 0.99 against a nominal 0.95, while the conditional variance alone covers 0.84.
Those quantities are therefore computed from posterior intervals, whose coverage across these processes measures between 0.94 and 0.97.
The posterior tier uses 12 replications per condition, fit in brms under the normal, sparse, bimodal, and exactly additive processes at 8, 32, and 128 groups.

The same tier checks sensitivity to the prior on $\sigma_\theta$.
Replacing the brms default, a half-$t(3, 0, 2.5)$, with a half-normal$(0, 1)$ changed root mean squared error by at most 0.001 under the normal, systematic, sparse, and bimodal processes at 8 and 128 groups.
With 2,000 observations the group standard deviation is identified well enough that any weakly informative prior gives the same answer.

\section{Comparison with Regularized Machine Learning}

While interactions are very common, researchers have other tools with which to regularize effect estimates. 
This section compares the hierarchical model with three of them with a LASSO applied to the saturated interaction expansion \citep{RatkovicTingley2017, BlackwellOlson2022}, a causal forest \citep{WagerAthey2018}, and Bayesian additive regression trees \citep{Dorieetal2022}.

All four estimators recover the same quantity, the treatment effect in each of the $2^k$ groups, so the comparison is on one estimand.
The LASSO is fit over the full interaction basis and penalized by cross-validation. The forest and the trees estimate an effect for each observation, which I average within group.
The data generating processes are those in the previous section, and each condition uses 50 Monte Carlo replications rather than the 200 used elsewhere, because a forest or a set of regression trees takes much longer to fit than a mixed model.

\begin{figure}[htpb]
	\centering
		\includegraphics[width=0.95\textwidth]{../figures/sim-ml-comp.png}
	\caption{Accuracy of group treatment effect estimates under partial pooling and three regularized alternatives. Bands give 95\% Monte Carlo intervals.}
	\label{fig:sim-ml-comp}
\end{figure}

\autoref{fig:sim-ml-comp} shows the result.
The three regularized estimators are similar and far more accurate than the fully crossed model. 
With 128 groups under the normal process, root mean squared error is 0.30 for the LASSO, 0.30 for the forest and 0.30 for the trees, against 0.55 for saturated ordinary least squares.

The hierarchical model is more accurate than all three in every condition, at 0.26 under the same setting.
Paired within replication, the differences run from 0.007 to 0.098 and exclude zero in all but three of the forty-eight comparisons.
The LASSO is the closest competitor because penalizing the interaction basis and pooling group deviations do similar things.
LASSO shrinks departures to zero and the hierarchical model shrinks them toward the mean.
The causal forest is consistently the least accurate of the three, likely due to a smaller sample.


There are two important qualifications.
First, the hierarchical advantage falls under sparse heterogeneity and few groups. 
With eight groups under the sparse process, the difference from Bayesian additive regression trees is 0.012 with an interval spanning zero, and the hierarchical model is more accurate in only 48\% of replications.


Second, and more important, this comparison is run on terms that suit the hierarchical model.
The modifiers are known in advance, binary, and the grouping is given.
Forests and regression trees are built to discover heterogeneity in high dimensional and continuous covariates. 
Therefore, when researchers already know which modifiers matter and want effects for interpretable groups, partial pooling estimates those effects more accurately than a generic regularizer.
Where the modifiers are unknown, machine learning tools are much better.

\section{Binning Continuous Modifiers}

Grouping observations on a continuous modifier requires choosing how many bins to use and where to place the cuts.
Both are researcher degrees of freedom that could hinder replication.
I provide some simulation guidance in this section to inform design and pre-registration.


Measuring this requires a different estimand.
With binary modifiers there is a finite set of groups, each with a true effect, and accuracy can be measured against those.
A continuous modifier has no groups until the analyst makes them, and every binning scheme implies a different set of true group effects. 
As a result, I cannot compare true to estimated without a circular analysis.
Accuracy is therefore measured against the true conditional effect at each respondent, $\tau(x_i)$, which does not depend on how the bins are drawn.

The simulation draws $x \sim \text{Uniform}(0,1)$ with $n = 2000$, and generates the outcome as in the previous sections.
I use three shapes for $\tau(x)$.
These are linear, a logistic curve, and a step function that jumps at $x = 0.5$.
Cuts are placed at equal widths, at quantiles of $x$, or at quantiles of the true effect.
That last rule is infeasible in practice, and I include it only as an upper bound on what better guidance could achieve.


I then compare five different approaches, including saturated least squares on the bins, partial pooling across bins treated as exchangeable, partial pooling that respects the ordering of the bins through a penalized spline on the bin index, and two estimators that never bin at all, one interacting the treatment with $x$ linearly and one fitting a smooth varying slope.
Each condition uses 100 Monte Carlo replications.

	\centering
		\includegraphics[width=0.95\textwidth]{../figures/sim-binning-comp.png}
	\caption{Accuracy against the true conditional effect under different binning schemes. The estimators that never bin do not vary with the number of bins and are repeated across each panel as a benchmark.}
	\label{fig:sim-binning}
\end{figure}



\subsection{Smoothly Varying Effects}

When $\tau(x)$ is linear or logistic, an estimator that keeps $x$ continuous is more accurate than any binning of it, at any number of bins.
Root mean squared error for the smooth varying slope is 0.064 under the linear process and 0.075 under the logistic one.
The best binned estimator reaches 0.069 and 0.078 respectively, and only at eight bins.
The gap is not large, but it never closes because when binning replaces a smooth function with a step function, it becomes less accurage.

This is a scope condition.
Grouping is worth its cost when the modifiers are genuinely categorical, or when researchers want effects for interpretable groups instead of a curve.
When the modifier is continuous and the grouping is not itself of interest, a varying slope on a smooth term is the better tool, it is just harder to integrate with discrete modifiers.

\subsection{Bin Count and Cell Size}

Comparing the two binned estimators is more useful than comparing them to the continuous ones, because it isolates what pooling does.
Under the linear process with quantile cuts, saturated least squares is most accurate at four bins (0.095) and then deteriorates, reaching 0.124 at eight bins as the cells empty out.
The hierarchical model improves monotonically over the same range, from 0.109 at two bins to 0.069 at eight.


Finer bins reduce the approximation error and shrink the cells, and which of those dominates depends on whether the estimator can handle cells with little data.
Least squares breaks down with many small cells, so it needs coarse bins that misrepresent the function. 
Partial pooling can handle finer bins, but inferences about individual bins should be treated with caution because those bins will be regularized..

\subsection{Cut Points}

The threshold process raises the largest concern about researcher degrees of freedom.
The true effect jumps at $x = 0.5$, so a cut point at 0.5 represents it perfectly and any other placement cannot.
With equal-width cuts the accuracy of the hierarchical model oscillates with the number of bins: 0.062 at two bins, 0.187 at three, 0.078 at four, 0.160 at five, and 0.096 at eight.
Even numbers of bins place a boundary at 0.5 and odd numbers do not.

Moving from two bins to three, a change most analysts would regard as innocuous, triples the error.
While cut points may seem rare, this is a clear argument for pre-registering a grouping scheme and reporting results under more than one.

\subsection{Ordered Bins}

Treating the bins as exchangeable discards information. Bins 1 and 5 are pulled toward the grand mean as strongly as bins 1 and 2 are pulled toward each other, when adjacent bins should be more alike.
A smoothing prior over the ordered bin index is the obvious remedy, but it does not work.

Under the linear and logistic processes the difference between the exchangeable and ordered versions is at most 0.002 in either direction, which is negligible.
Under the threshold process the ordered version is clearly worse, by 0.011 to 0.039 depending on the number of bins and the cut rule.
The reason is the same one that makes the threshold case hard. Smoothing across bins assumes that the effect varies gradually, and when the truth is a discontinuity that assumption is wrong in the place it matters most.


When a modifier is expected to shift the effect gradually, smoothing across ordered bins is defensible.
Where a discontinuity is plausible, exchangeable pooling is the safer default, because it imposes no belief about which bins are similar. 

\subsection{Practical Guidance}

These results translate into six recommendations.

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textit{Decide whether the groups are of interest.}
If the goal is how an effect changes along a continuous modifier, do not bin.
Fit a smooth varying slope instead, such as \texttt{s(x, by = treat)} in brms, or a Gaussian process \citep{Pratietal2023}.
Bin only when effects for interpretable groups are the quantity of interest, or when a continuous modifier must be combined with categorical ones.

\item \textit{Keep the trend in the systematic component and model deviation with bins.}
This leaves partial pooling to regularize some levels. 

\item \textit{Use more bins than least squares could support.}
Least squares was most accurate at four bins and deteriorated beyond that, while partial pooling kept improving through eight.
Before fitting, count the cells implied by crossing every grouping variable and check the smallest cell in each treatment arm, because crossing multiplies bins quickly.

\item \textit{Put cuts where theory or institutions put a threshold, or use quantiles.}
Adding bins does not help when a boundary is in the wrong spot.
Quantile cuts keep cells balanced when the modifier is skewed, where equal-width cuts leave the tail bins nearly empty.

\item \textit{Pool bins as exchangeable unless the effect should change gradually.}
Smoothing across ordered bins gained nothing where it was correct and cost accuracy where the effect jumped.

\item \textit{Pre-register the number of bins and the cut rule, and report at least one alternative.}
As with other issues, pre-registration can help express prior beliefs about these functions. 
Moreover, if pre-registered models do not fit well, that is informative, because model misspecification often complicates model fitting.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Barber and Pope Reanalysis}

\subsection{Data and Groups}

\citet{BarberPope2019} randomly assigned respondents to one of four conditions before asking their positions on ten policies.
In two, Trump was described as taking the liberal or the conservative position on each policy. In the third, Republican leaders in Congress took a position, and the fourth gave no cue.
Their analysis of heterogeneity compares the two Trump cues with the no-cue condition, and so does this reanalysis, which leaves out the party-leader condition.
Each respondent answered all ten items under a single condition.
After dropping respondents who answered ``not sure'' to the party identification question or skipped the Trump approval question, the data contain 7,173 item responses from 845 respondents.
That is the estimation sample of their Table 1, where the same respondents drop out through missing values on party strength and Trump approval.

The four modifiers are political knowledge, measured as the number of eight factual questions answered correctly, the seven-point party identification scale, Trump approval on a five-point scale, and self-placed ideology on a five-point scale.
Knowledge is nearly uncorrelated with the others, while party identification, Trump approval and ideology correlate between 0.62 and 0.68.
Their Table 1 uses a folded measure of party strength, estimated among independents and Republicans only.
Because the direction of a respondent's partisanship matters for a Trump cue, the models here use the full seven-point scale instead.

The groups for partial pooling cross party (Democrat, independent, Republican), Trump approval (disapprove, neither, approve), and knowledge (four or fewer correct answers, five or more).
That gives 18 groups, which range from 3 to 178 respondents, with a median of 22.

\subsection{Model and Code}

Each cue has its own heterogeneity equation, in which the four modifiers predict the effect of the cue and a varying intercept for each group is partially pooled.
The modifiers also enter the outcome equation, alongside a varying intercept for each group, one for each respondent, since each answered ten items, and one for each policy item.
The group intercept in the outcome equation matters.
Without it, groups that differ in baseline support can only show that difference through the cue slopes, which inflates the residual variation the model attributes to the cues.
In the paper's two-equation form, the brms formula is:

\begin{verbatim}
bf(
  Support ~ lamlib * libtrump + lamcon * contrump + ctrl,

  lamlib ~ knowledge + pid7 + trump_approve + ideo5b + (1 | grp),

  lamcon ~ knowledge + pid7 + trump_approve + ideo5b + (1 | grp),

  ctrl ~ knowledge + pid7 + trump_approve + ideo5b + race_white +
    (1 | grp) + (1 | caseid) + (1 | Question),

  nl = TRUE
)
\end{verbatim}

Multiplying out the two equations gives a single formula with the same parameters:

\begin{verbatim}
Support ~ (libtrump + contrump) *
    (knowledge + pid7 + trump_approve + ideo5b) +
  race_white + (1 | caseid) + (1 | Question) +
  (1 + libtrump + contrump || grp)
\end{verbatim}

The two are the same model, but with 845 respondent intercepts the non-linear version takes hours to sample and the multiplied-out version minutes, so the results reported here come from the second.
\autoref{tab:bp-equivalence} checks that the two give the same answer.
I ran that check on an earlier version of this model, before I added the group intercept to the outcome equation, and the two forms agree to three decimal places.

All coefficients receive $N(0, 1)$ priors, which are weakly informative on the scale of a binary outcome.
The standard deviations of the group, respondent, and item intercepts, and the residual standard deviation, take the brms default, a half-$t(3, 0, 2.5)$.

\subsection{Joint Tests}

In the interaction model that enters all four modifiers additively, none of Trump approval, ideology, or party identification is individually significant for either cue.
Tested jointly, the three moderate the liberal cue ($\chi^2_3 = 12.98$, $p = 0.005$) but only marginally the conservative cue ($\chi^2_3 = 6.87$, $p = 0.076$).
All four modifiers together are significant for both cues ($p = 0.005$ and $p = 0.030$).


\subsection{Group Variation and Robustness}

The standard deviation of the group-specific cue effects, net of the four modifiers, is 0.027 for the liberal cue (90\% interval 0.003 to 0.088) and 0.097 for the conservative cue (0.027 to 0.189).
The groups also differ in baseline support for these policies, with a standard deviation of 0.117 (0.079 to 0.173).
For the liberal cue, then, the four modifiers account for nearly all of the variation across groups, while for the conservative cue they leave a good deal of it unexplained.

Grouping by party and Trump approval alone, which gives nine groups, yields 0.044 and 0.115 with wider intervals.
The subgroup effects from that model correlate 0.997 with the main model's for the liberal cue and 0.975 for the conservative cue, and differ by at most 0.012 and 0.036.

The outcome is binary, and like the original analysis the main model uses a Gaussian likelihood.
Under a Bernoulli likelihood, with effects computed as the average change in the predicted probability of support, the subgroup effects correlate 0.997 with the main model's for the liberal cue and 0.900 for the conservative cue, and differ by at most 0.013 and 0.045.

\subsection{Out-of-Sample Accuracy}

To check whether one model estimates the subgroup effects Barber and Pope report more accurately than their separate models, I split the respondents at random into halves, stratified by group and condition, 200 times for each cue.
Using one half, each approach estimates the cue's effect among Democrats, independents and Republicans, among those who disapprove of Trump, neither approve nor disapprove, or approve, among low- and high-knowledge respondents, and among liberals, moderates and conservatives.
Those estimates are scored against the raw differences in means in the other half.
Because the held-out difference is independent and unbiased, its noise adds the same amount to every approach's error, and differences in the score are differences in mean squared error.
The hierarchical model is fit by restricted maximum likelihood, as in the simulations.

\section{Tomz and Weeks Reanalysis}

The section provides another demonstration of the hierarchical approach by reanalyzing a study by \citet{TomzWeeks2021}. 
Tomz and Weeks (TW hereafter) examine whether the public is more willing to go to war when the beneficiary of that intervention is a U.S. ally.
In a factorial experiment with vignettes, they find a 33\% average increase in support for military intervention on behalf of allies, compared to non-allies. 
This is a large and potentially important relationship because the United States has a global network of allies. 


Given the size of the main effect, TW's paper is an ideal scenario for comparing interactions and hierarchical models. 
Corresponding interaction effects may be large, and their sample size of 1,200 respondents is not unusual in published work. 
At the same time, TW estimated many interactions to check how other treatments modify the impact of alliances by fully crossing interactions of their experimental treatments.\footnote{This is one example of fully crossed interactions in applied research.}
There are 64 unique treatment groups with anywhere from 11 to 32 respondents, so estimates of the impact of alliances in the 32 pairs of alliance treatment and control groups employ at most 54 data points. 
As such, hierarchical regularization will likely change some conclusions by pooling noisy estimates in small groups.
For ease of comparison, I also compare an OLS model with additive instead of fully crossed interactions.
As the simulation results indicate, the fully crossed results are far noisier.



\subsection{Model and Code}

The alliance effect varies with the other four treatments (regime, stakes, costs, and region), which enter the heterogeneity equation additively.
The \verb+treat_group+ indicator marks the 32 combinations of those treatments and carries the varying intercept that is partially pooled.
The same four treatments also enter the outcome equation as main effects, alongside respondent demographics and foreign policy dispositions.
Costs are coded as in the original data, where one indicates low costs.

\begin{verbatim}
bf(
  force ~ lambda * alliance + controls,

  lambda ~ regime + stakes + costs + region_txt +
    (1 | treat_group),

  controls ~ regime + stakes + costs + region_txt +
    white + male + hawk + intl + dem + rep + age + ed4,

  nl = TRUE
)
\end{verbatim}

The main effects in the outcome equation are necessary, even though every treatment is randomized.
Without them, untreated respondents in every condition share one intercept, so each $\lambda_g$ estimates the difference between allied respondents in condition $g$ and non-allied respondents in all conditions.
That difference adds the condition's baseline support to its alliance effect.
Omitting them changes the answer substantially.
In the condition with the only negative fully crossed estimate, non-allied respondents support intervention at 0.90 against 0.47 across all conditions, so a model without main effects moves that estimate from $-0.15$ to 0.39.
Across all 32 conditions, its estimates correlate $-0.52$ with the additive least squares estimates, and leave-one-out cross-validation prefers the model with main effects by 35.3 points of expected log predictive density (standard error 9.4).
The same requirement applies to the Bush and Prather model below.

\subsection{Specification and Pooling}

I estimate the alliance effect in each of the 32 conditions three ways: with TW's fully crossed least squares model, with an additive least squares model that has the same modifiers and no pooling, and with the hierarchical model.
\autoref{fig:tw-het-treat-source} plots all three.

\begin{figure}[htpb]
	\centering
		\includegraphics[width=0.95\textwidth]{../figures/tw-het-treat-source.png}
	\caption{Estimated impact of alliance in each of the 32 experimental conditions, under fully crossed OLS, additive OLS, and the hierarchical model.}
	\label{fig:tw-het-treat-source}
\end{figure}

The additive model separates the two steps that distinguish the hierarchical model from TW's. Moving from the fully crossed to the additive model is specification, and moving from the additive to the hierarchical model is pooling.

Almost all of the change comes from the first step.
The standard deviation of the 32 estimates is 0.188 under the fully crossed model, 0.124 under the additive model, and 0.122 under the hierarchical model, and the hierarchical estimates correlate 0.997 with the additive ones.
Pooling adds little because the data support little variation beyond the additive structure. The posterior median of $\sigma_\theta$ is 0.031.
That estimate comes from the data rather than the prior, since it holds under the weakest prior considered.
In this application, then, the hierarchical model estimates that the condition-specific variation is small, where the additive model can only assume it.

The models rarely disagree about effect directions.
The fully crossed and hierarchical models assign the same sign to 31 of 32 conditions, and the additive and hierarchical models to all 32.
The exception is the only negative fully crossed estimate, $-0.154$ for a low-cost, high-stakes intervention on behalf of an Eastern European democracy, which the hierarchical model places at 0.070.

The models disagree much more about effect magnitudes.
For a high-cost, low-stakes intervention on behalf of a South American democracy, the fully crossed estimate is 0.048 and the hierarchical estimate 0.395.

\subsection{Sensitivity to the Prior, Likelihood, and Grouping}

Replacing the default prior on $\sigma_\theta$ with a half-normal$(0, 1)$ or half-normal$(0, 0.5)$ moves no condition estimate by more than 0.005, and the posterior median of $\sigma_\theta$ stays between 0.031 and 0.032.

A Bernoulli likelihood with a logit link changes the scale on which the modifiers combine, so its effects are computed as the average change in predicted probability within each condition.
Those estimates correlate 0.97 with the Gaussian ones and differ by at most 0.10.
Every condition has a positive estimate under both likelihoods, the smallest being 0.070 under the Gaussian model and 0.127 under the Bernoulli.
The usual weakness of the linear probability model shows up here, as 6\% of its expected values for individual respondents fall outside $[0, 1]$.
It does not distort the condition estimates, which the Bernoulli model reproduces closely.
As a posterior predictive check, the observed mean in each of the 64 alliance-by-condition cells falls inside its 90\% posterior predictive interval in 63 cells under the Gaussian model and 62 under the Bernoulli.

Grouping is a researcher choice, so I refit the model with region dropped from the heterogeneity equation, which leaves eight groups instead of 32.
The spread of the estimates is unchanged, with a standard deviation of 0.121 against 0.122.
Leave-one-out cross-validation cannot distinguish the two groupings. The 32-group model trails by 1.0 points of expected log predictive density, with a standard error of 1.9.
The conclusions do not depend on whether region defines the groups.

\subsection{Who Responds to Alliances?}


To further explore the potential application of hierarchical models, this section examines how demographic factors modify the impact of alliances.
I used party, news interest, race, and gender to modify the impact of alliances.
I selected these variables because Tomz and Weeks examine party and news interest as potential modifiers, but gender \citep{Barnhartetal2020} and race are also salient modifiers.
I control for other experimental manipulations, age and education.
Age and education are important group-level controls because they are group-level variables that are correlated across party, race and gender.
Following TW's OLS analysis, I use a Gaussian likelihood, although the outcome is a binary variable. 


The resulting hierarchical model thus encompasses four subgroup comparisons in TW's appendix while adding two more modifiers.
All of the comparisons in the hierarchical model would require at least six pairwise interaction models. 
What TW present in four figures, I show with two.



To start, I present the two regressions from the hierarchical model. 
\autoref{fig:tw-het-source} plots how different variables shape either the impact of an alliance or support for intervention. 
One facet of this figure gives the coefficient estimates from the model of heterogeneity, and a second gives the control estimates. 



\begin{figure}[htpb]
	\centering
		\includegraphics[width=0.95\textwidth]{../figures/tw-treat-het-source.png}
	\caption{Regression coefficients from a hierarchical model of how an alliance shapes public support for military intervention. The left facet reports predictors of group alliance effects, and the right facet the control variables in the equation with the alliance treatment. Points give the posterior median and error bars the 95\% credible interval.}
	\label{fig:tw-het-source}
\end{figure}


High news interest is the strongest predictor of how alliances impact support for war. 
Respondents with high news interest respond to alliances by .15 more than others in this survey, and that relationship could be as small as .05 or as large as .22.
This differs from TW's conclusion that respondents with high and low news interest respond in roughly the same way, likely because the hierarchical model accounts for other factors that are correlated with news interest.


Other demographic predictors do not differentiate responses to alliances as clearly. 
The impact of alliances may be somewhat greater for white and male respondents, but those differences are not clearly different from zero as the 95\% credible intervals include zero and small negative values.
Similarly, Republicans and Democrats are not all that different from independents in how they respond to alliances.
This matches TW's conclusion that partisanship does not create substantial differences in alliance effects.
The intercept in this equation is theoretically meaningful, as it captures the impact of alliances when all the modifiers are zero. 
For independent, non-white women with low news interest, alliances increase support for force by roughly .15. 


The control equation suggests that high stakes, high costs, and partner democracy all increase support for intervention.
Region is less consequential, as neither Asia, Eastern Europe nor Africa is clearly different from the reference category of Latin America.
Because neither education nor age are equal to zero, the intercept is not directly interpretable.


\begin{figure}[htpb]
	\centering
		\includegraphics[width=0.95\textwidth]{../figures/tw-treat-het-joint.png}
	\caption{Estimated impact of alliance on support for military intervention in different subgroups of respondents. Text gives the exact posterior median estimate.}
	\label{fig:tw-treat-het-joint}
\end{figure}



While it is possible to construct a rough profile of who responds most to alliances using the estimates in \autoref{fig:tw-het-source}, I provide a more precise summary in \autoref{fig:tw-treat-het-joint}. 
This figure plots the median estimate in each group of respondents, after grouping respondents based on race, gender, news interest and partisanship.
The median depends on the regression coefficients in \autoref{fig:tw-het-source}, with additional noise from the partial pooling components.
There are 24 unique estimates, all of which are clearly greater than zero. 
Alliances thus increase support for using force among all demographic groups, but the magnitude of the effect varies. 


Alliances most impact white Republican men with high interest in the news. 
In this group, the impact of alliances is .5, because this group was skeptical of intervention without an alliance rationale. 
The gap between high and low news interest in the other demographic groups is .15, which matches the coefficient in the heterogeneity equation.


The weakest impact of alliances appears for non-white, politically independent women with low news interest.
Here, alliances increase support for force by 13\%. 
White respondents respond by 8\% more, Democrats by 4\% more, and Republicans by 10\%. 
The accumulation of these different effects create substantial gaps between the groups.
